## 1. Research question and hypothesis

**RQ (plan §2.1).** When an action chunk is modified during execution, does
refreshing its predicted tactile outcome improve control compared with
retaining the original prediction?

**H1 (verbatim).** *After sufficiently large action corrections, forecasts
conditioned on the original action become less accurate than refreshed
forecasts, and this mismatch can reduce task success.*

* **H1-A**: correcting an action makes the original action-conditioned forecast
  less accurate than a refreshed forecast.
* **H1-B**: this forecast mismatch can reduce task success.

**Objective.** Determine whether the mismatch exists for a learned
action-conditioned contact predictor on held-out episodes; how it depends on
correction magnitude, type, and task phase; and whether it changes a closed-loop
controller's actions and outcomes when live contact feedback is also available.

## 2. Environment and task

`GripperLiftEnv` (`src/contact_learning/environments/gripper_lift.py`), MuJoCo 4.0.1.

* **Scene.** A gravity-compensated hand on a vertical slide joint
  (position servo with a ±30 N force limit) carries two prismatic fingers with
  box pads. A 4 cm box rests on a floor. Each episode samples box mass
  U(0.05, 0.20) kg and friction coefficient U(0.6, 1.0) from its seed. The box
  geom has contact priority, so its friction is the one used.
  Physics: 2 ms timestep, 20 Hz control, elliptic friction cones, noslip solver.
* **Action** (valid bounds `[-1, 0]`–`[1, 1]`).
  * `a[0]` is the grip command, `g = (a[0]+1)/2`. The finger position target is
    `g · 5 cm`, passed through a **slew limit of 0.06 per step** (a slow servo:
    a full stroke takes about 0.85 s).
  * `a[1]` is a lift velocity in [0, 1] × 0.25 m/s, ramped within each control
    period.
* **Contact observation** (`sensors/contact.py`): for each pad, the summed normal
  force and the magnitude of the summed tangential force between pad and box
  (from `mj_contactForce`), plus N(0, 0.02 N) noise. **This is an idealised net
  contact-wrench readout**, not a tactile array. It has no spatial
  distribution, hysteresis, latency, filtering, or calibration drift, and the
  contact softness (solref/solimp) was not fitted to any real material.
* **Proprioception:** finger positions, hand height, and applied (slewed) grip.
* **Task outcome:** the box is "held" if it is raised at least 5 cm and both
  pads report normal force > 0.1 N.
* **Nominal plan** (`controllers/scripted.py`): wait 2–6 steps, ramp the grip
  over 6–12 steps to `g_hold` ~ U(0.85, 0.98), hold 3–6 steps, then lift at
  0.4–1.0 × max speed to 8–14 cm.

### Environment validation (before any predictor work)

`results/h1/summaries/H1-001_env_validation.json`, figure `results/h1/figures/H1-001_env_validation.png`.

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Steady pad normal force vs. grip command, compared with the rigid model `kp·(g·5cm − 3cm)` | max deviation **2.7 mN** over g ∈ [0, 1]; zero force below contact (g < 0.6) |
| Held object: Σ tangential force vs. weight (20 seeds) | all 20 held; max relative error 8% (residual lift acceleration) |
| Coulomb prediction (held iff 2μN ≥ mg) vs. simulated outcome, 80 cases | **79/80 agree**; the single disagreement is at capacity ratio 1.009 |
| Reset determinism | bit-identical on repeat, and after another episode in between |

Three environment issues were found and fixed **before** any H1 trial:
1. The pad friction was being used instead of the box friction. Fixed with
   geom priority.
2. A per-step jump in the height target produced impulsive accelerations that
   shook the box loose. Fixed with a per-substep ramp.
3. Allowing downward lift let the stiff hand servo press the pads onto a
   dropped box, producing non-physical ~70 N "contacts" that made up 94% of
   forecast squared error. Fixed by making lift one-directional and limiting
   the servo force.

These were design fixes made from inspecting physics and data, not from H1
outcomes.

## 3. Predictors

Inputs and outputs are explicit in `src/contact_learning/predictors/features.py`.
At decision time *t*, a predictor reads:

* contact `obs[t-3..t]` (4×4)
* proprioception `obs[t-3..t]` (4×4)
* past actions `a[t-4..t-1]` (4×2)
* the candidate future actions `a[t..t+9]` (10×2)

and predicts contact `obs[t+1..t+10]` (10×4). Rows before the episode start
are padded with the first observation and the rest action.

| Model | Description |
|---|---|
| `persistence` | trivial baseline: every future step = last measured contact |
| `mlp_history_only` | same MLP as below **without** future actions (action-blind negative control) |
| `ridge` | linear action-conditioned model (closed-form ridge, α = 1, residual on persistence) |
| `mlp` (primary) | residual MLP, 3×256 SiLU, AdamW lr 1e-3, cosine schedule, ≤ 40 epochs, early stopping on validation RMSE (patience 6) |

**Training data** (per predictor seed *s* ∈ {0,…,4}): 2000 training and 300
validation episodes, each from its own disjoint seed range. Each episode is a
random nominal plan plus Poisson(1.5) random corrections (random type, start,
length 3–15 steps, magnitude U(0, 0.5)) and N(0, 0.02) action noise. That gives
102,000 training windows. Corrections are deliberately included in training so
that refreshed forecasts are not pure extrapolation. **Predictor test:** 300
further episodes from a disjoint seed range, shared by all seeds.
Leakage checks: seed-range disjointness is asserted at runtime, and a test
verifies the history builder never reads rows beyond *t*.

**Held-out predictor-test RMSE (N), 5 seeds:**

| Model | Mean ± SD over seeds |
|---|---|
| persistence | 0.522 (deterministic) |
| mlp_history_only | 0.235 ± 0.001 |
| ridge | 0.286 ± 0.000 |
| **mlp** | **0.144 ± 0.002** |

The MLP beats persistence on 94.7–95.0% of test episodes, for every seed. It
also beats the history-only MLP for every seed, so it does use the future
actions. Its train RMSE (0.123–0.129) is close to its validation RMSE
(0.126–0.151): no gross overfitting.
Figure: `results/h1/figures/H1-001_predictor_quality.png`.

## 4. H1-A protocol

As registered (see `docs/experimental_protocol.md`, H1-A). Code: `src/contact_learning/evaluation/h1a.py`.

* **Trials:** 2100 held-out episode seeds `50,000,000 … 50,002,099`, one correction each.
* **Independent variables:**
  * correction type ∈ {grip −, grip +, lift −, lift +}, uniform;
  * requested magnitude ∈ {0, .02, .05, .1, .2, .3, .5}, uniform. Analysis uses
    the *effective* magnitude after clipping to action bounds.
  * correction time: 50% stratified so that the nominal contact onset falls in
    the forecast window, 50% uniform.
* **Controlled:** the same history vector for both forecasts (byte-identical,
  tested); the same horizon H = 10; the same executed trajectory `Y_corr` for
  scoring; the same noise stream for `Y_orig` and `Y_corr`.
* **Derived labels:**
  * `phase`: whether the nominal grasp-contact state changes within
    [t_c−2, t_c+H] ("near_transition"), contact holds throughout
    ("stable_contact"), or neither ("no_contact");
  * `contact_changing`: whether the binary grasp-contact sequence differs
    between executing `A_corr` and `A_orig`;
  * `outcome_change_rmse`: RMSE between `Y_corr` and `Y_orig`.
* **Metrics** (`evaluation/metrics.py`):
  * `E` = RMSE over the 10×4 forecast entries vs. measured `Y_corr` (N);
  * `D = E_cached − E_refreshed` (positive means refreshing helps);
  * `D_sham`: refresh with a *different*, never-executed correction of the same
    requested magnitude (negative control);
  * per-horizon RMSE, noise-free-target RMSE, and contact-state error;
  * `forecast_shift` = RMSE(refreshed, cached), i.e. how much the predictor
    responds to the correction;
  * single-forecast latency.
* **Statistics:** the unit is a trial. Per-trial metrics are averaged over the
  5 predictor seeds, then reported per magnitude bin with a trial-bootstrap
  95% CI (2000 resamples), Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and Holm correction
  across the non-zero bins. Per-seed estimates are reported as well.

**Diagnosed anomaly.** Large *effective* grip-increase corrections
(≥ 0.15, n = 59) show D ≈ 0 or slightly negative. All 59 occur about 7 steps
before contact onset. The commanded grip changes, but the slew-limited fingers
do not reach the box within the horizon. The realized change is therefore
≈ 0 (median 0.000 N), while the predictor still shifts its forecast
(median 0.05 N). **Magnitude measured in command space is therefore a poor
proxy for the change in contact.**

## 6. H1-B protocol

As registered (see `docs/experimental_protocol.md`, H1-B), including the
amendment made before the run (a fix for refresh double-counting). Code:
`src/contact_learning/evaluation/h1b.py`.

* **Trials:** 200 per magnitude × 7 magnitudes = 1400 trials, from seeds
  `60,000,000 + 100,000·i_m + j`. Three candidate seeds were skipped because no
  correction time had nominal grasp contact.
* **Runs:** every trial is run under conditions A, B, and D with each of the
  5 predictor seeds, and once under C and E. That is 23,800 episodes.
* **Scenario:** a persistent grip-decrease correction of magnitude
  m ∈ {0, .05, .1, .15, .2, .25, .3}, applied at a chunk boundary
  t_c ∈ {10, 20, 30} where nominal grasp contact exists. A downstream safety
  controller keeps measured and predicted mean pad normal force ≥ 1.7 N.
  * Live term (all conditions, every step): `u_l += max(0, 1.7 − N_meas)/7.5`.
  * Forecast term: `u_f += max(0, 1.7 − min_k N̂_k)/7.5`.
* **Conditions:**
  * A cached: forecasts at chunk boundaries, made before the correction is known
  * B refreshed: + one event-triggered forecast at t_c that replaces the stale one
  * C live-only
  * D fixed-rate: forecasts every 5 steps, on a schedule independent of the correction
  * E oracle: true simulated future; not deployable
* **Success:** box raised ≥ 5 cm and both pads in contact at T = 60 (3 s).

**Does the forecast change the controller's actions?** Yes. The summed absolute
grip difference from A, Σ_t |g_B − g_A|, rises with magnitude: 0.05, 0.38,
1.09, 1.71, 1.91, and 2.94 for m = .05 to .30. For comparison, live-only differs
from A by ≤ 0.015 and fixed-rate by ≤ 0.084. The minimum measured normal force
after t_c, at m = .30, is 0.50 N for A, C, and D, and 1.87 N for B.

≈ 1.8 ms (simulation). B used fewer forecasts than D and did better, so B's
gain does not come from extra computation. Under these conditions it comes
from the *timing* and *action-conditioning* of the update.

**Where failures happen.** All failures under A occur at t_c ∈ {20, 30}.
In 75% of them the lift is in progress at t_c.

## 8. Statistical uncertainty

* H1-A CIs are trial-bootstrap intervals over 2100 independent trials (one per
  episode seed). Seed-to-seed variation of the main estimate is small
  (per-seed mean D for ≥ 0.15 ranges from 0.301 to 0.313).
* Training-data variation is included, because each seed has its own
  training episodes. Test trials are shared across seeds.
* H1-B failures are rare events. At m ≤ 0.15 there are too few discordant
  pairs (≤ 3) to detect a difference.
* No hyperparameters, thresholds, or bins were changed after seeing results.
  The one amendment (an H1-B bug fix) preceded the full run.

## 9. Limitations

* **Simulation and sensor fidelity.**
  * The "tactile" signal is an exact MuJoCo net contact force plus small
    Gaussian noise, with no sensor latency, filtering, or spatial structure.
  * Contact softness and friction are not calibrated to any real material.
  * One object shape. Mass and friction ranges are narrow.
  * The SO-101 and its tactile sensor are not modelled.
* **Task simplicity.** The dynamics are close to quasi-static and nearly
  linear in the grip command (N ≈ 7.5·(g − 0.6) N). That makes the
  action → contact map easy to learn. Richer contact (rotation, multi-point,
  deformables) may behave differently.
* **H1-A is partly expected by construction.** An accurate action-sensitive
  predictor must disagree with itself when the action changes the outcome.
  The non-trivial findings are about *where* this does and doesn't happen.
* **Predictor sensitivity is uneven.** The MLP responds weakly to lift
  corrections, although lift increases change the realized contact by ~0.19 N.
  Refreshing therefore helps little there. This is a property of this
  predictor and this dataset.
* **The H1-B scenario was chosen because staleness could matter there.**
  It has a slow, slew-limited gripper; a persistent grip-decrease correction;
  and a safety layer that largely *undoes* the correction. That last part is
  artificial: a real upstream correction usually exists for a reason. A
  different controller (for example, one that clamps the commanded grip to a
  known safe minimum) could avoid the failure without any learned forecast.
  This was not tested.
* **The live-feedback baseline has zero sensing latency.** Even so, it fails at
  large magnitudes, because the actuator is slower than the force drop. With
  sensing latency, live feedback would be weaker still. With a faster
  actuator, it might suffice.
* **The cached forecast is never *worse* than having no forecast.** In this
  controller, a stale forecast is simply ignored once live feedback
  takes over. The mismatch therefore costs only the opportunity to
  anticipate, and does not mislead the controller. A controller that trusts
  forecasts more than measurements (the H2 setting) could behave differently.
* **Single task and one predictor architecture.** No real-robot data.

## 10. Alternative explanations considered

| Alternative | Assessment |
|---|---|
| D > 0 is an artifact of pairing or leakage | Ruled out by the tests: identical history bytes, exact zero-correction equivalence, action-blind D ≡ 0, the sham refresh is *worse* than cached, and bit-exact replays. |
| The learned predictor is too weak, or the effect is predictor-specific | The MLP beats persistence by 3.6× and the history-only model by 1.6×. Ridge shows the same direction with a smaller D. The effect size depends on predictor action-sensitivity, especially for lift. |
| B's control gain comes from extra computation | B made 4.5 forecasts per episode vs. 7.0 for D (fixed-rate), and D gained nothing. |
| B's gain comes from newer observations | All conditions get identical live measurements every step. B differs only in conditioning on the corrected action at t_c. |
| B's gain could come from *any* action-aware feed-forward, not specifically a learned tactile forecast | Plausible. This is not distinguished here, and it is an important point for the contribution claim. |
| The failures are caused by sensor noise or execution delay (plan §3) | Noise σ = 0.02 N is about 1% of the force scale. The relevant delay is actuator slew, which is part of the mechanism rather than a confound. Sensing delay was not modelled. |

## 11. Conclusion

* **H1-A: preliminary support** under the tested conditions. All registered
  criteria are met: the predictor beats the baselines; for corrections ≥ 0.15,
  D = +0.305 N [0.266, 0.344], positive in 5/5 seeds; zero-correction
  equivalence is exact; the negative control is negative.
  * The effect appears even at the smallest correction bin (≤ 0.035,
    D = +0.025 N). It tracks the realized contact change (r = 0.91), not the
    commanded magnitude.
  * It is confined to correction directions the predictor is sensitive to
    (grip, not lift).
  * When a correction barely changes contact, refreshing is neutral to very
    slightly harmful.
* **H1-B: preliminary support, narrow.** In a slow-gripper grasp-and-lift
  scenario, refreshing the forecast at the correction raises success from
  0.835 to 0.998 at m = 0.3. The gain is significant at m ≥ 0.2 in all seeds.
  * Live-only control and fixed-rate (5-step) refreshing perform the same as
    the stale forecast. The value comes from conditioning on the correction
    *at the moment it happens*.
  * The stale forecast did not harm control relative to having no forecast.
  * Whether a learned forecast beats a simple analytic feed-forward rule was
    not tested.
