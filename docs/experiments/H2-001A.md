# H2-001A — Local causal forecast-interference experiment: report

*Simulation only (MuJoCo, CPU). Run 2026-11-20 on an Apple M5 (10 cores), with
no GPU. The protocol was registered before any main training or held-out
evaluation: [`H2-001A_PROTOCOL.md`](H2-001A_PROTOCOL.md), commit `f671f95`.
Readable summary: [`../H2_001A_RESULTS.md`](../H2_001A_RESULTS.md).*

## 1. Bottom line

* **Is H2 supported?** In this setting the registered verdict is
  **contradicted**: forecast dependence was weak, and refreshing changed
  recovery by less than δ (§9 of the protocol).
* **The forecast was stale.** Ten steps after a disturbance, the cached
  forecast's error was about 7× its nominal level (**M1 established**).
* **Staleness did not cause worse actions or worse recovery (M3 and M4 not
  established).**
  * Paired probes held state, live touch, and context identical and changed
    only the forecast. Swapping the stale forecast for a fresh one moved the
    grip command by 0.003 on average. That is below the registered 0.01
    threshold, and it moved the action slightly *away* from the teacher's.
  * Refreshing changed recovery by −0.9 points [−2.9, +1.0].
* **Discrepancy-triggered gating did nothing measurable.** It was within δ of
  both the cached forecast and matched random gating.
* **Taking the forecast away hurt.** Suppressing it lowered recovery by 4.5
  points [−6.9, −2.4]. The policy's actions depend on the forecast only
  weakly, but the forecast is net *helpful*, even when stale.
* **The only harmful forecast-pathway effect ran the other way.** Refreshing
  *more often* than the policy was trained for caused over-squeezing (crushing)
  in nominal episodes for one of five seeds (§5.6).
* **A simple reactive controller beat every learned condition.**
  * It recovered 99.7% of seen-type disturbances vs. 90.5% for the learned
    policy, and 74% vs. 22% on the unseen friction drop.
  * Its cost: about 32% more squeeze force.
  * The live-only learned student matched the forecast-conditioned one.

## 2. What was implemented

Everything is in `src/contact_learning/h2a/`; H1 code is untouched.

* **Environment** (`env.py`). The H1 gripper scene (subclassed, not modified)
  gets:
  * a fragile object with a crush limit;
  * a two-segment lift plan;
  * three disturbance types, all through simulator dynamics:
    * a downward load and a lateral push via `xfrc_applied` on the object,
      ramped over 0.4 s;
    * a gradual friction drop.
  * Severity is the post-onset required per-pad normal force divided by the
    crush limit.
  * A test checks that disturbed and nominal tactile streams are bit-identical
    until onset.
* **Teacher** (`expert.py`). A privileged grip-force controller, with margin
  1.4 and anticipation of the lift load. Non-deployable.
* **Forecaster.** MLP predicting 20 steps of pad forces under a held-grip
  assumption. Trained on nominal counterfactual rollouts.
* **Students.**
  * **P** (forecast-conditioned): its forecast is chunk-cached, with 20%
    forecast dropout.
  * **D** (live-only).
  * Both are trained by DAgger on identical budgets: 5 iterations × 600
    episodes, 2.4 × 10⁵ labelled steps each.
* **Harness** (`runner.py`, `evaluate.py`).
  * Every refresh and gating rule.
  * Branching at onset from a bit-identical state.
  * Paired action probes with refreshed, null, shuffled, and oracle forecasts.
  * A Mahalanobis OOD check on the policy's last hidden layer.
* **Statistics** (`stats.py`). Crossed bootstrap over student seeds × episodes,
  with episode-only and seed-only intervals.
* **Scripts.**
  * `experiments/h2/h2_001a.py` (train, calibrate, evaluate, benchmark);
  * `experiments/h2/analyze_h2_001a.py` (the registered analysis);
  * configs `configs/experiments/h2_001a.yaml` and `h2_001a_smoke.yaml`.
* **Tests.** `tests/test_h2a.py` (11 tests). The full suite is 44 tests, all
  passing.

## 5. Mechanism analysis (protocol §9)

### 5.1 M1, forecast mismatch: **established**

Forecast error is the mean absolute error of the forecast in use, over the 10
steps after onset:

| Latency | A cached, disturbed | A, nominal (same window) | A − B (paired) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 ms | 0.122 N [0.114, 0.131] | 0.018 N [0.017, 0.019] | **+0.063 N** [0.053, 0.073] |
| 150 ms | 0.147 N [0.134, 0.160] | 0.019 N [0.018, 0.020] | +0.023 N [0.015, 0.030] |

The forecaster was trained on nominal data, so even a refreshed forecast stays
about 3× its nominal error after a disturbance.

### 5.2 M2, forecast dependence: **weak** (50 ms) / **absent** (150 ms)

* **Nominal action sensitivity at lift-off** (|g_cached − g_null|):
  * 0.001 grip units [0.001, 0.002] at both latencies;
  * registered threshold 0.01 (≈ 0.075 N);
  * refreshing at lift-off changes the action by 0.007 on average.
* **Behaviourally,** suppression never changed nominal success. It did lower
  recovery:
  * −4.5 points at 50 ms;
  * −2.6 points [−5.1, −0.2] at 150 ms, below δ.

So the policy uses the forecast a little, and the effect is beneficial. It is
not "meaningful" under the registered rule.

### 5.3 M3, action interference: **not established**

Paired probes on the 10 post-onset steps of the A branch, at 50 ms, on seen
types:

| Forecast swapped in | Mean action change | Interference (\|g_A − g\*\| − \|g_variant − g\*\|) | Under-grip component |
|---|---|---|---|
| refreshed | 0.003 | **−0.001** [−0.002, −0.001] | −0.002 |
| null | 0.003 | −0.001 | −0.001 |
| shuffled (another episode's) | 0.004 | −0.001 | −0.000 |
| oracle* | 0.012 | −0.005 | −0.003 |

What this shows:

* **Negative interference** means the stale-forecast action was slightly
  *closer* to the teacher's than the refreshed-forecast action. Swapping in a
  completely wrong forecast (shuffle) moved the action about as much as the
  correct refresh. The action is barely forecast-specific.
* **The 150 ms results are the same:** refresh change 0.002, interference
  −0.001.

### 5.4 M4, recovery degradation: **not established**

| Latency | B − A |
|---|---|
| 50 ms | −0.009 [−0.029, +0.010] |
| 150 ms | −0.025 [−0.055, +0.005] |

The direction is, if anything, opposite to H2.

### 5.5 Distribution-shift diagnostics

The table gives the fraction of probe hidden states beyond the 99th percentile
of nominal development states.

| Forecast variant | Nominal lift-off probes | Post-disturbance probes |
|---|---|---|
| cached (unchanged input) | 2.7% | 19% |
| refreshed | **21%** | 18% |
| null | 3.0% | 20% |
| shuffled | 26% | 22% |
| oracle* | 24% | 43% |

* **The disturbance itself** moves about 19% of states out of distribution,
  whatever forecast is used.
* **A refreshed forecast at a non-chunk step** is out of distribution in
  nominal conditions (21% vs. 2.7%). The policy only ever saw age-0 forecasts
  at chunk boundaries.
* **The oracle's clean, exact forecasts** are the most out of distribution.
  That explains why the oracle *lowers* recovery (−12.8 points [−22.7, −4.1]):
  better information delivered through a shifted input distribution hurts.

### 5.6 The one harmful forecast-pathway effect: off-schedule refreshing

* **What happened.** Nominal success fell for B (−6.1 points [−19.2, 0.0]) and
  E (−5.9 points) relative to A at 50 ms. Both are registered secondary
  contrasts.
* **Where.** Almost entirely seed 4: nominal success 0.695 (B) and 0.705 (E),
  vs. 1.000 for A. Every one of the 138 nominal B/E failures across all seeds
  and latencies was a **crush**, never a drop.
* **At 150 ms** the same seed fell to 0.945 under B.
* **What it means.** Updating a learned policy's forecast more often than it
  was trained for can push it into an unusual input region, where it
  over-squeezes. This is interference from *fresh* forecasts, not stale ones.
  It is seed-specific and was not hypothesised.

### 5.7 Selective gating

* **Threshold.** θ was the 99th percentile of nominal per-step discrepancy:
  0.70 N at 50 ms, 0.56–0.58 N at 150 ms. It was chosen on development episodes
  without looking at outcomes.
* **It discriminated poorly.**
  * **Nominal episodes:** at least one trigger in 23.6% of them (0.3% of
    nominal steps).
  * **Disturbed episodes:** at 50 ms, it fired in only 5.9% of them, with a
    median first trigger 12 steps after onset; at 150 ms, in 28%.
* **Why.** Nominal discrepancy peaks at lift-off, where the held-grip forecast
  is least accurate. The 0.4 s disturbance ramps build discrepancy slowly.
* **Consequence.** F made 3.4 refreshes per post-onset window, vs. 3.0 for A,
  and was indistinguishable from A and from G at a matched budget.

The suppress variant Fn was equivalent too.

## 6. Secondary populations

**Unseen disturbance type (friction drop, recoverable):**

* The learned policies recover only 20–26% of episodes, under every forecast
  condition (all contrasts with A are within ±δ).
* R recovers 74%, and the teacher 100%.
* The students do not generalise to a disturbance type absent from training.
  The forecast pathway neither helps nor hurts.

**150 ms latency, seen types:**

| Controller | Recovery |
|---|---|
| A | 0.741 |
| B | 0.717 |
| C | 0.715 |
| D (live-only) | **0.776**; D − A = +0.035 [+0.007, +0.061] |
| R | 0.997 |

With delayed touch, the forecast-conditioned student is slightly *worse* than
the live-only one.

**By severity** (50 ms, seen types; recovery for A, C, D, R):

| Severity | A | C | D | R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.55–0.65 | 0.97 | 0.97 | 0.98 | 0.99 |
| 0.65–0.75 | 0.96 | 0.90 | 0.94 | 1.00 |
| 0.75–0.85 | 0.80 | 0.73 | 0.83 | 1.00 |

The learned policies' failures concentrate in the most severe recoverable
disturbances.

**Unrecoverable episodes (severity > 1):** 0% success for every controller,
including the teacher, as designed. No condition "rescues" a physically
impossible episode.

**Recovery time** was ≈ 0 for almost every recovered episode, because
recoveries happened without measurable slip. Recovery is decided by whether
the grip catches up before the object slides 12 mm. The metric is reported in
`compute_and_secondary_metrics.csv` but is uninformative here.

|---|---|
| A | 8.0 |
| B | 39.6 |

| F and G (matched) | 8.4 |

## 8. Answers to the six mechanism questions

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does forecast mismatch occur after a disturbance? | **Yes.** About 7× nominal error; refreshing halves it at 50 ms. |
| 2 | Does the policy meaningfully depend on the forecast? | **No.** Only weakly at 50 ms, and not at all by the registered rule at 150 ms. Removing it costs a few points of recovery, but actions barely move. |
| 3 | Does stale information cause worse actions? | **No.** Stale-forecast actions are, if anything, marginally closer to the teacher's. |
| 4 | Does it lower recovery? | **No.** B − A is within ±δ at both latencies, with point estimates favouring the stale cache. |
| 5 | Does selective gating help without nominal harm? | **No benefit**, and no nominal harm. It is not better than random gating at a matched budget. The trigger rarely fired after real disturbances. |
| 6 | Forecasting, or a simpler reactive controller? | **A simple reactive controller wins outright** (+9 to +52 points). It pays for that with about 32% more grip force than the economical learned students. The learned live-only student matches the forecast-conditioned one at 50 ms and beats it at 150 ms. |

## 9. Limitations

* **Simulation only.** The tactile signal is an idealised net-force proxy with
  additive noise. It is not a sensor, and nothing here transfers to the SO-101
  without new evidence.
* **One controller class.** A small MLP trained by DAgger from a privileged
  teacher. It learned to rely mostly on live touch, proprioception, and the
  weight prior.
  * The result says stale forecasts do not interfere *in a policy that does
    not need them*.
  * It does not test pretrained world-action models such as TacPAC, whose
    actions are generated jointly with predicted touch. Their dependence on
    the forecast is structural, not learned from optional inputs.
  * Per the brief, no dependence was forced, so this is the honest outcome
    for this class.
* **One task.** Grasp, lift, and hold with three disturbance types. The task
  turned out to be easy for reactive control once a weight prior is
  available.
* **Development choices (protocol §10).** The weight prior, the 0.4 s ramps,
  and the margin were set on development seeds to make the learned policies
  nominally adequate. Different choices could change how much the forecast
  matters.
* **The forecaster was trained on nominal data only.** Its refreshed forecasts
  after a disturbance are extrapolations: a refreshed forecast still had 3×
  nominal error.
* **The gating threshold rule** (nominal 99th percentile) was conservative and
  poorly discriminating. A rule tuned for disturbance detection on development
  data might trigger more, but cannot add value while actions are insensitive
  to the forecast.
* **The off-schedule-refresh harm (§5.6)** is driven mainly by one seed out of
  five, and is a secondary, unhypothesised finding.

## 10. Recommendation

**Revise H2; do not reject it.**

* **What this rejects.** For a learned controller that treats the forecast as
  an optional input, H2-001A is a clean negative: stale forecasts did not
  interfere, and a simple reactive rule was better.
* **What it leaves open.** H2 remains open for architectures where action
  generation is coupled to predicted touch. That is the case the ManiFeel /
  TacPAC-structured route (H2-001) was designed for, and it should resume
  when GPU access is available.
* **What to measure first there.** The policy's forecast dependence (M2),
  before any interference experiment.

**Most informative next step** (H2-001B, local and cheap): study the one
interference H2-001A *did* find.

* Vary the refresh schedule at test time vs. training time (chunk-trained vs.
  refresh-trained vs. mixed-age-trained students).
* Measure crush and drop rates and OOD distances.

This feeds H3 directly: any "selective update rule" must not move a learned
policy off its training distribution of forecast ages. This experiment showed
that this can matter more than staleness.
